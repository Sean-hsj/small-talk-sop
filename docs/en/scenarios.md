# English workplace small talk — practice scenarios

[← Home](../../README.md) · [Core SOP](sop.md) · [Role matrix](role-scenario-matrix.md) · [One-minute card](quick-reference.md)

Use these for solo rehearsal, pair role-play, manager training, or review. Read the setup, try your own response, then compare. The “good move” is one safe option, not the only correct line.

## 1. Headphones at a desk

**Setup:** A colleague is typing with headphones on. You have no urgent work need.

**Good move:** Do not interrupt. If they look up, smile or say “Morning” and keep moving.

**Why:** Focus is a stronger signal than your wish to be friendly.

**Avoid:** Waving until they remove headphones, touching the desk, or asking, “Busy?”

## 2. New colleague in the kitchen

**You:** “Hi, I don’t think we’ve met—I’m Noor from Finance.”

**Them:** “I’m Daniel. I started Monday.”

**You:** “Welcome. How are you finding the first week?”

**Them:** “Good, just a lot to take in.”

**You:** “That makes sense. If you need the room-booking guide, I can send it. I’ll let you get your coffee.”

**Why:** Name, context, one easy question, one useful offer, clean exit.

## 3. A first name may not be the right title

**You:** “Hi, I’m Alex. I’ve heard people call you both Dr. Nair and Priya—which do you prefer?”

**Them:** “Priya is fine.”

**You:** “Great, thank you.”

**Avoid:** Assuming informality from nationality, age, or what one colleague calls them.

## 4. Meeting starts in two minutes

**You, to the whole group:** “Has anyone tried the café downstairs yet?”

One person answers; another stays quiet. When the host starts, you stop.

**Why:** The prompt is inclusive and optional; silence does not become conspicuous.

**Avoid:** “Let’s go around and make everyone share their weekend.”

## 5. Elevator with a senior executive

**You:** “Morning, Elena. I liked your point about onboarding in the town hall.”

**Them:** “Thank you.”

**You:** “Have a good morning.”

**Why:** Their short reply closes the loop. You do not convert an enclosed 20-second encounter into a pitch.

## 6. Shared event creates a thread

**You:** “That automation demo was interesting. What did you think?”

**Them:** “The error handling looked useful. We have the same problem.”

**You:** “That part stood out to me too. Are you solving it manually now?”

**Them:** “Mostly.”

**You:** “Sounds familiar. I’ll let you enjoy the break—good talking with you.”

## 7. Two short answers

**You:** “How’s your week going?”

**Them:** “Fine.”

**You:** “Got it. It’s nice and quiet in here today.”

**Them:** “Yeah.”

**Good move:** “I’ll let you get settled. See you later.”

**Avoid:** Launching question three. Two low-investment responses are enough to reduce intensity.

## 8. The real purpose is work

**Good:** “Hi Mei—quick question about the release notes. Do you have five minutes now, or would later be better?”

**Avoid:** “Hi” → “How was your weekend?” → ten lines of chat → surprise urgent request.

**Why:** A friendly opening should not conceal a demand.

## 9. Remote colleague in another time zone

**Message:** “Hi Luis—no rush on this. How did the customer workshop go? I was curious whether the new format worked.”

If Luis reacts with 👍 only, treat it as complete.

**Avoid:** Repeated mentions, “Are you there?”, or reading delayed response as dislike.

## 10. Camera is off

**Host:** “We’ll start in two minutes. Feel free to chat, type in chat, or just settle in quietly. Cameras are optional.”

**Avoid:** “Come on, show your face so we know you’re engaged.”

**Why:** Disability, bandwidth, caregiving, space, culture, and concentration can all affect camera use.

## 11. You did not understand an accent

**You:** “Sorry, I missed the last part. Could you say that again a little more slowly?”

**Then:** “Just to check, you said Thursday, right?”

**Avoid:** Pretending, laughing, imitating the sound, or saying “Your accent is so strong.”

## 12. An English learner gives a textbook answer

**Them:** “I am fine, thank you. And you?”

**You:** “Good, thanks. A busy morning, but not bad.” Continue normally.

**Avoid:** Correcting them, praising their English as surprising, or switching languages without asking.

## 13. Weekend question reveals difficulty

**You:** “Anything planned for the weekend?”

**Them:** “I’ll be at the hospital with my dad.”

**You:** “I’m sorry. I hope everything goes as smoothly as possible.”

If you have a relevant work role: “Is there anything practical we should adjust at work?”

**Avoid:** Asking the diagnosis or telling a competing family story.

## 14. Colleague returns from leave

**You:** “Good to see you back. Would a quick catch-up help, or would you rather settle in first?”

**Avoid:** “Did you have a nice break?” or “What exactly happened?”

## 15. A colleague mentions a same-gender partner

**Them:** “My wife and I went to the coast.”

**You:** “Nice—what was your favorite part?”

**Why:** Follow the volunteered travel topic and the person’s word. Do not make identity into a special interview.

**Avoid:** “I didn’t know you were gay!” or sharing the information with others.

## 16. Someone appears pregnant

**Good move:** Say nothing about pregnancy. Use an ordinary greeting.

If they announce it: “Congratulations!” Then follow their lead.

**Avoid:** Guessing, asking the due date before disclosure, touching their body, or discussing leave plans unless it is your formal role and the right setting.

## 17. You used the wrong pronoun

**You:** “He—sorry, she—sent the final version.” Continue.

If the error was repeated or public, apologize privately and change the behavior.

**Avoid:** A long apology that requires reassurance or questions about identity history.

## 18. Gossip begins

**Them:** “Did you hear why Chris is really off work?”

**You:** “No, and that sounds like Chris’s story to share. Do we need to reassign anything on the project?”

**Why:** Refuse speculation and move any real work need into an explicit question.

## 19. A client introduces politics

**Client:** “So what do you think about the election?”

**You:** “I try not to get into party politics at work. I did enjoy hearing about the industry policy panel, though. Shall we get started?”

**Avoid:** Assuming agreement because the client has power or arguing to prove authenticity.

## 20. Alcohol pressure at a team event

**Them:** “Why aren’t you drinking? Come on, just one.”

**You:** “I’m good with this, thanks.”

If repeated: “I don’t discuss the reason. Let’s drop it.”

**Manager or bystander:** “They’ve chosen their drink. Let’s move on.”

**Avoid:** Inventing a medical reason you do not want to share. A refusal is complete.

## 21. Declining a social event

**Invitation:** “A few of us are going for lunch at 12:30. Feel free to join us if you’d like.”

**Decline:** “Thanks for inviting me. I’m sitting this one out—have a good time.”

**Response:** “Of course. See you this afternoon.”

**Avoid:** “Why not?” or allowing decisions to happen only at the event.

## 22. Manager asks about dating

**Manager:** “Are you seeing anyone?”

**Employee options:**

- “I keep that private.”
- “I don’t discuss dating at work.”
- “I need to get back to the report.”

**Good manager repair:** “Sorry—that was too personal. You don’t need to answer.” Then change topic and do not repeat it.

## 23. No eye contact, but engaged

Your colleague looks at their notes while giving detailed answers and asking relevant questions.

**Good move:** Continue based on verbal reciprocity. Do not demand eye contact or comment on it.

**Why:** Eye contact is not a universal measure of respect or interest.

## 24. Noisy lunch with a hard-of-hearing colleague

**You:** “Would a quieter table or written chat be easier?”

Ask the person, then follow the answer. Face them when speaking if that is their preference.

**Avoid:** Speaking only to their companion, shouting without asking, or discussing their hearing with the group.

## 25. A joke lands badly

**You:** “That comment wasn’t appropriate. I’m sorry.” Stop.

**Avoid:** “It was only a joke,” “You’re too sensitive,” or asking the target to explain identity and humor to everyone.

## 26. Repeated sexual or identity-based comments

**Good move:** Treat this as possible harassment, not a phrase-selection problem. Prioritize safety, record facts appropriately, and use company policy, HR/employee relations, union or employee representation, ethics, or relevant external channels.

The target does not have to personally confront or educate the person first.

## 27. A personal invitation is declined indirectly

**Senior colleague:** “Would you like to get a drink with me, just the two of us?”

**Junior colleague:** “This week’s complicated. Maybe another time.”

**Good move:** Treat the vague postponement as a close. Do not invite them again, switch from work chat to a personal account, ask coworkers whether they are interested, or change their work opportunities.

**Why:** Power can make a direct “no” costly. One non-acceptance is enough.

## 28. Team-event photo

**Organizer:** “We’re planning a group photo for the public company page. I’ll check with everyone privately beforehand; joining is completely optional.”

Before posting or tagging, confirm the stated use and each person’s permission. Being at the event or accidentally appearing in frame is not consent to publication.

## 29. A colleague uses AAC or typing

Your colleague types a response on an augmentative and alternative communication device.

**Good move:** Address them directly, wait without finishing the sentence, and continue at their pace. If the setting is unclear, ask: “Would you rather talk, type, or use chat?”

**Avoid:** Speaking only to a companion, assuming reduced understanding, or rushing because the response takes longer.

## 30. Monday with a peer

**You:** “How was your weekend?”

**Them:** “Good. Mostly stayed home.”

**You:** “Nice. Mine was quiet too. I’ll let you get settled.”

**Why:** A broad answer stays broad. Do not ask who they live with, whether they are single, or why they did not go out.

## 31. A colleague has mentioned a child

**Them:** “My daughter had her first football match.”

**You:** “That’s a big first. How did she find it?”

**Avoid:** School cost, grades, custody, parenting judgment, or asking when they will have another child.

## 32. One-to-one lunch with your manager

**Before lunch:** “Is this mainly a casual lunch, or should I bring anything for a work discussion?”

The manager introduces unexpected performance feedback.

**You:** “I’d like to give this proper attention. Could we continue it in our scheduled one-to-one?”

**Why:** Food and informality should not remove normal feedback structure.

## 33. Manager invites a direct report to lunch

**Manager:** “I’m getting lunch at 12:30. You’re welcome to join, but it is optional and not a performance conversation.”

**Employee:** “Thanks. I’m taking a quiet lunch today.”

**Manager:** “Of course. See you this afternoon.”

Important decisions still return to the team channel.

## 34. Coffee queue with your manager +2

**You:** “Good morning. I found the customer example in the town hall useful.”

**Senior leader:** “Glad to hear it.”

**You:** “Enjoy your coffee—I’ll let you get on.”

**Avoid:** A surprise promotion pitch, complaint, or request for an immediate decision.

## 35. Deputy and matrix managers disagree

You meet one manager during a break.

**Good move:** “I have two different priority instructions. I’ll set up a short work conversation with both of you rather than use the break.”

**Avoid:** Asking which manager has more power or using casual remarks as authorization.

## 36. Three-person lunch becomes a two-person story

Two colleagues keep saying, “Remember when…” while the third has no context.

**Good move:** “We should give Maya the context. We were talking about last year’s launch. Maya, have you seen the new version?”

**Avoid:** Putting Maya on the spot with a personal question merely to compensate.

## 37. Age stereotype around technology

**Risky:** “This new system must be hard for people your age.”

**Better:** “How is the new system working for you?” Ask the same question across the team.

Experience, speed, and preference are individual. Do not assume ability from age.

## 38. Cross-gender mentoring and “optics”

A manager offers informal mentoring to men but avoids women because one-to-one meetings might “look wrong.”

**Good move:** Offer equal access with transparent scheduling, professional locations, a clear purpose, and the same boundaries for everyone.

**Why:** Safety is not achieved by excluding a gender from development.

## 39. Return from leave when the reason is unknown

**You:** “Good to see you back. Would you like the quick update, or some time to settle in?”

**Avoid:** “How was your vacation?” The absence may have involved health, grief, caregiving, or another private reason.

## Facilitator scorecard

After a role-play, score 0–2 for each:

- checked timing, privacy, power, and channel;
- used a low-assumption opener;
- listened to one thread instead of interrogating;
- matched depth and shared proportionally;
- noticed non-expansion and accepted the first boundary;
- offered a real exit;
- protected identity, privacy, and work access;
- used natural, simple English.

Total: 16. A score below 13 needs another attempt. Any coercion, discriminatory content, outing, repeated intrusive question, or retaliation is an automatic fail.
