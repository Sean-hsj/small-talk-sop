# English role and scenario matrix

Use this after the core [English SOP](sop.md). It helps choose the right depth for a specific person, moment, and group size.

This is a decision aid, not a demographic script. Do not infer gender, age, relationship status, pregnancy, parenthood, health, or sexuality from appearance.

## 1. Five-question lookup

Before choosing a line, answer in order:

1. **Power:** Who controls work, evaluation, pay, access, or opportunity?
2. **Setting:** Is it public, private, enclosed, online, or after hours?
3. **Purpose:** Is this social, developmental, operational, or a hidden work request?
4. **Group size:** One-to-one, two or three people, a group, or a formal meal?
5. **Known context:** What has this person voluntarily shared in this setting?

Power and setting come before age, gender, or family context. When facts are unknown, use the neutral default.

## 2. Role and power matrix

| Role | Default aim | Good starting range | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Peer on your team | Easy future coordination | Shared work context, food, commute, public interests | Question chains, gossip, assumed friendship |
| Peer on another team | Build a bridge without extracting information | Shared project, event, process, public expertise | Fishing for confidential details |
| New or less-established peer | Reduce uncertainty | Practical orientation, names, easy local information | Treating them like a child or testing fit |
| Your direct report | Recognition plus psychological room | Workload, resources, voluntary interests | Friendly questions feeling compulsory |
| Your manager | Human connection without managing upward theatrically | Shared context, public priorities, light interests | Compliments becoming flattery or a hidden ask |
| Manager +1 or +2 | Brief recognition and context | Public talk, company event, visible shared issue | Elevator pitch, complaint dump, career ambush |
| Deputy or assistant manager | Respect the actual remit | Shared work, team interface, current context | Treating them as a messenger to the “real” manager |
| Dotted-line or matrix manager | Clarify which role is active | Shared program, priorities, collaboration | Mixing social warmth with conflicting instructions |
| Mentor or sponsor | Maintain the agreed developmental relationship | Learning, career interests, shared professional context | Assuming personal friendship or unlimited access |

### 2.1 Peer on the same team

**Do**

- Use shared context and roughly equal speaking time.
- Remember details they chose to share.
- Keep private friendship information out of work groups.

**Say**

- “How’s your side of the week going?”
- “Have you found a good lunch option around here?”
- “That meeting was a lot. Want to decompress for a minute or get back to work?”

**Avoid**

- Running through home, salary, relationship, and weekend questions.
- Turning every answer into advice.
- Using one colleague as a source of gossip about another.

### 2.2 Peer on another team

**Do**

- Name the shared connection.
- Keep commercial or operational requests explicit.
- Appreciate expertise without asking for restricted detail.

**Say**

- “We both seem to touch the onboarding process. How is it working on your side?”
- “I liked your point in the review. Have you worked in this area long?”
- “This is partly a work question, so I’ll be direct.”

**Avoid**

- “Off the record, what is your team really planning?”
- Using social rapport to bypass the person’s manager or process.

### 2.3 Your direct report

Your words carry evaluation pressure even when your tone is casual.

**Do**

- Ask the same neutral workload questions across the team.
- Offer social invitations with a credible no.
- keep career feedback, scheduling, and performance decisions explicit.

**Say**

- “How’s the workload this week? Anything I should help reprioritize?”
- “I’m getting lunch at 12:30. Feel free to join; either way, project decisions stay in the team channel.”
- “This is a casual check-in. There is no need to discuss your time away.”

**Avoid**

- Dating, pregnancy, family plans, medical detail, or home circumstances.
- Using small talk to discover after-hours availability.
- Rewarding social participation or penalizing quietness.

### 2.4 Your manager

**Do**

- Keep spontaneous contact brief when they are moving or preparing.
- Use specific, sincere appreciation only when relevant.
- State a work ask early instead of disguising it as rapport.

**Say**

- “Morning, Priya. How’s your day going?”
- “Your explanation of the trade-off helped. I used that structure in my notes.”
- “I have one work question when you have five minutes.”

**Avoid**

- Agreeing with every comment.
- Performing personal closeness in front of others.
- Raising a complex complaint in an elevator or lunch queue.

### 2.5 Manager +1 or +2

Examples include your manager’s manager, the next level above, a department head, or a senior executive.

**Do**

- Assume the encounter will stay brief.
- Refer to public context, not private rumors.
- Move substantive ideas into a scheduled channel.

**Say**

- “Good morning. I found the customer example in the town hall useful.”
- “I have a related idea, but this is not the moment. May I send a short note?”
- “Nice to see you. I’ll let you get to your meeting.”

**Avoid**

- A surprise promotion pitch.
- Criticizing your manager to their manager.
- Asking the senior person to resolve something before hearing context.

### 2.6 Deputy, assistant, vice, or matrix manager

Titles vary. “Vice manager” may mean deputy manager, assistant manager, vice president, or a translated local title. Confirm the person’s actual remit.

**Do**

- Treat their authority as real where it applies.
- Ask which channel owns a conflicting request.
- Address them directly, not as a route to someone more senior.

**Say**

- “For this program, should I align with you or with Jordan on final priority?”
- “How is the transition between the two teams going?”
- “I’ll send the work detail separately so this coffee break stays a break.”

**Avoid**

- “Can you pass this to the real manager?”
- Comparing two managers’ power or popularity as small talk.

## 3. Gender and identity: what changes and what does not

Gender does not determine a topic list. The baseline stays the same across women, men, non-binary people, trans people, and people whose identity you do not know.

| Situation | What changes | What does not change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity is unknown | Use the name or neutral language | Do not guess from voice, body, clothing, or partner |
| Pronouns are volunteered | Use them accurately | Do not request an identity explanation |
| A partner is mentioned | Mirror “partner,” “wife,” or “husband” | Do not infer sexuality or ask who plays which role |
| Pregnancy is disclosed | Congratulate briefly and follow their lead | Do not ask medical, fertility, or body questions |
| One-to-one mentorship across genders | Use the same professional access rules | Do not deny mentorship because of gender |
| Private or enclosed setting | Increase clarity, distance, and exit access | Do not assume same-gender contact is automatically safe |
| Social invitation | One invitation; accept any non-acceptance | Friendliness is not romantic interest |

### 3.1 Compliments

Prefer action, judgment, craft, or impact:

- “You made that decision much clearer.”
- “Your questions helped the group slow down.”
- “That handover was very thorough.”

Do not make appearance, body, age, clothing fit, attractiveness, masculinity, or femininity the basis of routine workplace warmth.

### 3.2 Equal access without forced intimacy

- Offer mentoring, travel, visibility, and informal information consistently across genders.
- Use transparent locations, agendas, and channels when power or privacy risk is high.
- Do not solve harassment risk by excluding one gender from career access.
- Follow company policy for reporting lines, relationships, travel, and conflicts.

## 4. Age and life-stage context

Age bands are poor conversation instructions. Use only information the person has volunteered and only at the depth they opened.

| Known context | Safe follow-up | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New graduate or early-career status is known | “How are you finding the transition?” | “You’re just a kid,” dating questions, generation jokes |
| Person is older or more experienced | Ask about expertise or current interests | Health, retirement, grandchildren, technology assumptions |
| Person mentions a partner | Follow the activity they mentioned | Marriage timeline, gender roles, private relationship quality |
| Person mentions a child | Ask one child-neutral follow-up if they engage | Second-child plans, school cost, fertility, parenting judgment |
| Person mentions caregiving | Acknowledge; ask about work support if it is your role | Diagnosis, family conflict, why another relative cannot help |
| Person mentions living alone | Follow the actual topic | Loneliness, dating, safety, or financial assumptions |
| Person announces pregnancy or parental leave | Congratulate and follow their lead | Guessing dates, body comments, medical questions |
| Person mentions retirement plans | Ask what they are looking forward to | “Aren’t you too young/old?” or succession gossip |
| No life-stage detail is known | Use shared context and neutral plans | “Married?” “Kids?” “How old are you?” |

### 4.1 Children and family

If a colleague says, “My son started school,” a proportional reply is:

> “That sounds like a big week. How is he finding it?”

One answer does not open school fees, grades, custody, fertility, spouse roles, or future children.

If you have no family information, ask:

> “Anything planned for the weekend?”

Do not say:

> “What are you and the kids doing?”

### 4.2 Similar age is not automatic closeness

Sharing a generation, university era, or life stage can make a topic easier. It does not remove workplace, identity, romantic, or privacy boundaries.

## 5. Moment and occasion matrix

| Moment | Safe opener | Read this signal | Clean exit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday or after a weekend | “How was your weekend?” | A broad answer means stay broad | “Hope the week starts smoothly.” |
| After a public holiday | “Did you get any time to recharge?” | Family or travel may be sensitive | “Good to have you back.” |
| Returning from vacation | “Welcome back. Any highlight you feel like sharing?” | Do not assume leisure, money, or happiness | “I’ll let you settle in.” |
| Returning from unexplained leave | “Good to see you. Want the quick update or time to settle?” | Do not call it vacation | “Reach out when you’re ready.” |
| Coffee queue | “Have you tried this place before?” | The line ending ends the talk | “Enjoy your coffee.” |
| Before lunch | “Have you decided where to eat?” | Food can involve health or religion | “Enjoy lunch.” |
| After lunch | “How was the place?” | Do not comment on portion or body | “See you at the afternoon meeting.” |
| Casual break | “How’s the day going?” | Headphones or task return close it | “I’ll let you enjoy the break.” |
| Before a meeting | “How’s everyone’s morning?” | Include the whole visible group | Stop when the host starts |
| After a difficult meeting | “That was intense. Want a minute or some space?” | Do not force emotional processing | “I’ll give you some room.” |
| After a successful presentation | “Your example made the point clear.” | Keep praise specific | “Congratulations—see you later.” |
| During a deadline | “Quick hello—I know you’re in focus mode.” | Default to very brief | “Good luck. I’ll get out of your way.” |
| End of shift | “How did the shift go?” | Fatigue means no follow-up required | “Get some rest. See you next time.” |
| Remote Monday | “Morning. No rush—how’s your week starting?” | A reaction can close it | “Talk later.” |

### 5.1 The first day back is not always a happy return

Vacation, parental leave, bereavement, illness, caregiving, suspension, and unexplained absence require different assumptions.

When the reason is unknown, use:

> “Good to see you back. Would you like the quick update, or some time to settle in?”

## 6. Meal size and purpose matrix

First classify the meal:

- **Social lunch:** optional relationship time
- **Lunch meeting:** work with food; agenda and attendance rules apply
- **Developmental meal:** career or mentoring purpose stated in advance
- **Client meal:** professional hospitality and compliance rules apply
- **Team dinner:** work-related social setting; normal workplace boundaries continue

| Format | Useful topics | Structure | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-to-one with a peer | Shared interests, work journey, current context | Balanced questions and sharing | Accidental date, oversharing, gossip |
| Two or three peers | Inclusive shared topics | Rotate attention; explain inside context | Two people forming a closed pair |
| Group social lunch | Food, public events, light shared experiences | Optional joining and leaving | Round-robin disclosure, status clustering |
| One-to-one with your manager | Stated casual or developmental purpose | Clarify whether work topics are expected | Surprise feedback, personal pressure |
| One-to-one with your direct report | Resources and voluntary interests | Manager makes exit and cost rules clear | Attendance feeling required |
| Skip-level lunch | Company experience and public priorities | State purpose; keep reporting lines intact | Complaint extraction, loyalty testing |
| Lunch meeting | Agenda topics plus brief warm opening | Notes and decisions return to work channels | Pretending mandatory work is social |
| Client or vendor meal | Public interests, event, travel logistics | Compliance, cost, and purpose clear | Alcohol pressure, confidential comparison |
| Team dinner with alcohol | Shared public topics and voluntary stories | Equal non-alcoholic options; easy departure | Coercion, teasing, career access after hours |

### 6.1 One-to-one lunch with a peer

**Open**

> “Glad we could get lunch. How’s your week been outside the immediate deadline?”

**Keep safe**

- Share as much as you ask.
- Do not assume the meal is romantic.
- Keep absent colleagues’ private lives out of it.

**Close**

> “This was nice. I need to head back for the two o’clock.”

### 6.2 One-to-one lunch with your manager

Clarify purpose before the meal:

> “Is this mainly a casual lunch, or should I bring anything for a work discussion?”

If feedback appears unexpectedly:

> “I’d like to give this proper attention. Could we put the feedback in a scheduled one-to-one?”

The manager should not use a casual meal to ask about dating, family plans, health, loyalty, or after-hours availability.

### 6.3 One-to-one lunch with your direct report

The manager should state:

> “This is optional and not a performance conversation. If you prefer, we can keep our regular one-to-one instead.”

Paying rules depend on company policy and culture. Clarify cost in advance so the invitation does not create obligation.

### 6.4 Two or three colleagues

Use topics all can enter. When two people share history, give the third person context without forcing them to perform.

> “We were talking about the new café. Have either of you tried it?”

Avoid “Do you remember when…” for ten minutes while one person sits outside the story.

### 6.5 Group lunch or lunch meeting

For a social lunch:

- Attendance is optional.
- People may leave without explaining.
- Important decisions return to official channels.

For a lunch meeting:

- Call it a meeting.
- Send an agenda.
- Provide food and accessibility information.
- Count the time as work where policy requires.

Do not label mandatory work as “just lunch” to remove normal meeting protections.

## 7. Fast combinations

| Combination | Better move |
| --- | --- |
| Peer + coffee queue | Shared observation, one question, end when served |
| Peer + Monday | Ask broadly; follow only a volunteered detail |
| Direct report + return from leave | Welcome, offer work catch-up, no personal reason |
| Manager + one-to-one lunch | Clarify purpose; move surprise feedback to a real meeting |
| Manager +2 + elevator | One public-context line and a clean exit |
| Deputy manager + casual break | Respect actual remit; keep instructions out of the break |
| Three peers + lunch | Rotate attention; prevent a two-person inside conversation |
| Group lunch + newcomer | Give context and an optional entry, not a personal spotlight |
| Mixed-gender travel + dinner | Same boundaries; transparent plan, easy exit, no romantic inference |
| Older colleague + technology topic | Ask about current practice; do not assume difficulty |
| Younger colleague + social event | Invite without calling them “the young one” or assigning nightlife |
| Colleague mentions children | Follow one volunteered detail; do not expand into fertility or parenting |

## 8. Final neutral defaults

When role, identity, or life stage is uncertain:

1. Use the person’s name and shared context.
2. Ask one question that can be answered in one line.
3. Keep the same respect across genders and ages.
4. Treat volunteered detail as a narrow, one-time opening.
5. Increase caution with power, privacy, alcohol, travel, or one-to-one settings.
6. End before the other person must reject you.
