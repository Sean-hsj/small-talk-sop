# Workplace Small Talk SOP — English

[← Home](../../README.md) · [Role matrix](role-scenario-matrix.md) · [Manager add-on](manager-style-addon.md) · [One-minute card](quick-reference.md)

[Practice scenarios](scenarios.md) · [Cross-cultural guide](../cross-cultural-guide.md) · [Sources](../../SOURCES.md)

Default environment: international workplaces, multinational teams, and foreign companies operating in China. This is not a universal “Western culture” script. Adapt to the person, team, company, region, and applicable policy.

## 1. The outcome

Good workplace small talk leaves both people:

- recognized, not inspected;
- a little more comfortable working together;
- free to continue, change topic, or leave;
- with no new obligation, secret, or social debt.

Success is not a long conversation. A smile, “Morning,” and “Have a good one” can be complete. Do not measure success by how much personal information you learned.

## 2. Start here: SAFE

Use **SAFE** for almost every exchange:

1. **Scan** the moment, setting, power, privacy, and signals.
2. **Ask lightly** from shared context, with room for a short answer.
3. **Follow** one thread: acknowledge, optionally ask once, and share a little.
4. **Exit cleanly** before the exchange becomes an obligation.

Typical length:

- Passing greeting: 5–20 seconds
- Elevator or pre-meeting: 20–90 seconds
- Kitchen or lunch: 1–5 minutes when both people engage
- Scheduled coffee: longer by agreement; it is no longer spontaneous small talk

These are guides, not timers. Engagement matters more than duration.

## 3. Scan before you speak

### 3.1 Timing signals

| Signal | Default action |
| --- | --- |
| Open posture, looking around, waiting, relaxed pace | Green: greet and try one light opener |
| Walking but not rushing, arranging materials, checking phone occasionally | Yellow: use a short greeting; let them decide whether to expand |
| Headphones, deep focus, active call, eating alone while working, closed door, urgent pace, visible distress | Red: nod or say “Hi” only; do not demand attention |

Do not require eye contact, smiling, quick speech, or enthusiastic tone. Culture, disability, neurotype, pain, concentration, and energy all affect those signals. Look for a cluster, especially whether the person expands or reciprocates.

### 3.2 The five checks

Ask yourself:

1. **Moment:** Do they appear available?
2. **Place:** Could an answer expose them in front of others?
3. **Power:** Could my role make this feel mandatory?
4. **History:** Has this person welcomed this topic before, or am I guessing?
5. **Channel:** Is this appropriate here, or would it create noise or a written record they did not choose?

If unsure, lower the intensity: greeting → shared observation → optional question → no question.

### 3.3 The power multiplier

Managers, interviewers, mentors, senior leaders, clients, and anyone controlling work or opportunity must use stricter rules:

- prefer work-adjacent or shared-context topics;
- disclose lightly before asking for personal information;
- state that attendance and answers are optional;
- accept the first short answer without follow-up;
- do not turn social disclosure into performance, staffing, scheduling, or promotion data;
- never ask a group to reveal personal facts as a compulsory “icebreaker.”

Better manager opener: “I’m taking a quick coffee break. You’re welcome to join, but no pressure—I know people recharge differently.”

## 4. Ask lightly

### 4.1 Observe, ask, and share

You do not need all three every time.

- **Observe:** “It’s much quieter in here than yesterday.”
- **Ask:** “How’s your morning going?”
- **Reveal:** “I’m still waking up—this is coffee number one.”

Start with something you both share. Ask one easy question. If they engage, acknowledge what they said, ask one related follow-up, and add one short sentence of your own. Contributing a little prevents the exchange from feeling like data collection.

### 4.2 Reliable opener families

#### Greeting plus temperature check

- “Morning, Sam. How’s it going?”
- “Hi, good to see you. How’s your week been?”
- “Hey—how are things going?”

“How are you?” can be a ritual rather than a medical question. A natural brief answer is: “Good, thanks—how about you?” You may also say, “A bit busy, but okay.” No one owes a detailed or positive answer.

#### Shared situation

- “Have you tried the new coffee machine yet?”
- “That was a useful demo. What did you think?”
- “Looks like half the team had the same idea about coming in today.”

#### Work-adjacent, not a hidden work request

- “Been working on anything interesting lately?”
- “How did you get interested in this field?”
- “I liked the way you explained that chart. Have you worked much with data visualization?”

If you need a decision, update, favor, or deadline, say so directly rather than disguising it as small talk.

#### Leisure with an opt-out

- “Anything planned for the weekend?”
- “Have you found any good lunch spots around here?”
- “I’m looking for a new podcast. Heard anything good lately?”

Avoid assuming money, travel, free weekends, family structure, physical ability, diet, or alcohol use.

#### Reconnecting

- “Good to see you back. Would you like the quick version, or some time to settle in first?”
- “It’s been a while. How have things been?”

After leave, illness, bereavement, or an unexplained absence, do not ask “Did you have a nice break?” Follow what the person has chosen to disclose.

### 4.3 Make questions easy to answer

Prefer questions that are:

- low stakes;
- relevant to the current context;
- open enough for personality, but narrow enough not to feel like an interview;
- possible to answer in one line;
- free of hidden assumptions.

Compare:

| Risky | Better |
| --- | --- |
| “Where are you really from?” | “Are you based in this office, or do you work remotely?” |
| “Do you have a husband/wife?” | Do not ask by default. If they mention a partner, use their word: “your partner.” |
| “Why don’t you drink?” | “Would you like something alcoholic or non-alcoholic?” Then accept the answer. |
| “What happened to you?” | Do not infer a condition. If setup is part of your role, ask everyone: “Would anything make this setup work better for you?” |
| “When are you having kids?” | Do not ask. |
| “You look tired—are you sick?” | Do not replace one appearance inference with another. Use an ordinary greeting. If workload check-ins are part of your role, ask everyone the same neutral work question. |
| “What does your name mean?” | “Could you help me pronounce your name correctly?” |

### 4.4 Use names and titles accurately

- Listen to introductions and mirror the chosen level of formality.
- Ask once: “Could you say your name again so I get it right?”
- Do not shorten, translate, nickname, or anglicize a name without permission.
- In cultures and professions that use titles, ask: “Do you prefer Dr. Chen or Lian?”
- If you forget: “I’m sorry—could you remind me of your name?” Do not avoid the person for weeks.

## 5. Follow without interrogating

### 5.1 The one-thread rule

Choose one detail from the answer, not a new category.

> A: “I tried a pottery class this weekend.”
>
> B: “That sounds fun. What did you make?”
>
> A: “A very uneven bowl.”
>
> B: “Honestly, that’s the ideal first bowl. I tried drawing last year and everything leaned left.”

B acknowledges, asks one related question, then shares. B does not jump to where, with whom, how much it cost, or why A went alone.

### 5.2 Acknowledge, ask once, and share briefly

- **Acknowledge the meaning:** “That sounds like a big week.”
- **Ask once, if invited:** “What part are you most excited about?”
- **Share proportionally:** “I know the feeling—I’m presenting on Thursday too.”

Match depth, not exact content. If someone says “Weekend was fine,” do not answer with a deeply personal story to force intimacy.

### 5.3 Listen for continuation

Continue when several signals appear:

- answer contains detail beyond what was required;
- person asks you a question;
- person introduces a new but related thread;
- posture and pace remain available;
- conversation survives a natural pause without you pushing.

Exit or reduce intensity when several of these appear:

- one-word or repeated generic answers;
- no question or detail returned;
- attention returns to screen, door, clock, or task;
- delayed or minimal chat replies;
- topic change, nervous laugh, hedging, or “anyway…”;
- the person says they are busy, tired, private, or not in the mood.

One signal can be ambiguous. Repeated non-expansion is enough. Do not make them refuse directly.

### 5.4 Silence is allowed

After asking, wait. Do not answer for them or add three more questions. In a second language, noisy room, or neurodivergent interaction, processing may take longer.

If silence becomes awkward:

- “No rush—I was just saying hello.”
- “Anyway, I hope your morning goes smoothly.”
- “I’ll let you get settled.”

## 6. Exit cleanly

Good exits protect the other person and end with warmth.

### 6.1 Universal formula

**Reason or transition + positive close**

- “I’m going to refill my water. Good talking with you.”
- “I’ll let you get back to it—good luck with the presentation.”
- “I need to join my next call. Catch you later.”
- “I won’t keep you. Hope the rest of your day is calmer.”

You do not need an elaborate excuse. Do not promise a coffee or follow-up you do not intend to arrange.

### 6.2 When you want to leave

- “Good catching up—I need to get back to this.”
- “I’m going to take a quiet break, but I’ll see you this afternoon.”
- “I’m going to take a quiet break. See you later.”

### 6.3 When they want to leave

Release them immediately:

- “Of course—see you later.”
- “No problem. Hope it goes well.”

Never add a final personal question while they are leaving.

## 7. Topic ladder

The same topic can move up or down based on relationship, context, and the other person’s lead.

### Level 1 — Low pressure by default

- greeting and the immediate shared environment;
- noncontroversial work-adjacent interests;
- tools, learning, books, podcasts, entertainment, or public events;
- food options without commenting on bodies or diets;
- weather or commute without assuming where someone lives;
- voluntary plans framed broadly;
- appreciation of a specific action or skill.

Specific appreciation is better than appearance commentary: “Your explanation made that decision much clearer” beats “You look great today.”

### Level 2 — Follow only after they open it

- partner, children, parents, or caregiving;
- hometown, cultural background, or language;
- housing, travel cost, personal spending;
- health, disability, grief, burnout, or leave;
- diet, fitness, weight change, or body;
- salary and career moves;
- religion or personal celebrations;
- dating and social life.

Permission is topic-specific and revocable. A colleague mentioning their child once is not an invitation to ask about fertility, custody, school costs, or parenting choices.

### Level 3 — Do not initiate as small talk

- pregnancy, fertility, family planning, age, medical diagnosis, medication;
- sexual orientation, gender history, immigration status, ethnicity, caste, or “real” origin;
- voting, partisan politics, religious belief, or polarizing conflict;
- salary, debt, inheritance, or financial hardship in a power-imbalanced setting;
- sex life, romantic availability, or comments that could be flirting;
- body, attractiveness, clothing fit, weight, hair texture, skin, or disability;
- rumors, performance information, confidential work, or another person’s private life;
- jokes based on protected or marginalized identity;
- reasons for not drinking, eating, attending, making eye contact, or joining after-hours events.

If the other person raises a Level 3 topic, you may listen without probing. In a manager, HR, or reporting role, move out of small-talk mode and follow policy.

## 8. Situation-specific procedures

For combinations of power, identity safeguards, moment, meal purpose, and group size, use the [role × scenario matrix](role-scenario-matrix.md).

For an optional, non-diagnostic way to calibrate length, order, pace, detail, and channel with a manager, use the [manager communication-style add-on](manager-style-addon.md).

### 8.1 First day or first meeting

Goal: reduce uncertainty, not test personality.

1. Introduce your name, role, and connection.
2. Offer one practical piece of context.
3. Ask one easy, relevant question.
4. Release them.

“Hi, I’m Jordan from product operations. I work with your team on launches. The quiet rooms are at the end of this hall, in case you need one. How’s your first morning going?”

Avoid asking a new hire to tell the group a surprising fact, relationship status, dream, fear, or embarrassing story.

### 8.2 Hallway, elevator, or kitchen

- Use a greeting or shared observation.
- Keep your body from blocking the route.
- Ask at most one question unless they clearly expand.
- End when the physical moment ends.

Elevators are enclosed; do not use intimacy, flirting, criticism, or a request that requires an answer before the door opens.

### 8.3 Before a meeting

- Include the whole visible group, not only the highest-status or most familiar person.
- Prefer a question anyone may answer: “Has anyone tried the café downstairs?”
- Avoid weekend round-robins that force disclosure.
- When the meeting starts, stop cleanly; do not make late participants feel they interrupted a private club.

### 8.4 Remote and asynchronous work

Remote teams need social connection, but written small talk can become permanent, interruptive, or compulsory.

- Put the work purpose first when urgency matters: “Quick question about the release notes…”
- For a social message, make response optional: “No need to reply now—how did the conference go?”
- Use a social channel, thread, or status convention where available.
- Do not send repeated “hello?” messages to make someone respond.
- Treat emoji or a brief reaction as a complete acknowledgment.
- Do not read response speed, camera use, home background, or online status as friendliness or commitment.
- Do not screenshot or forward casual messages without permission.
- Before taking, recording, posting, forwarding, or tagging a workplace photo or video, obtain separate permission. Attendance or being in frame is not consent to publication. For group photos, state where the image will be used and provide a no-photo option without singling anyone out.

Good: “Morning! No rush—I saw the hiking photo you shared in the team channel. That view looked incredible.”

Risky: “Hi” → wait → “Are you there?” → personal question, when the real purpose is a work request.

### 8.5 Video meetings

- Do not demand cameras for social warmth unless policy and accessibility support it.
- Use a time-bounded opener: “We’ll start in two minutes; feel free to chat or settle in quietly.”
- Avoid comments on rooms, family members, sounds, or appearance.
- Give people multiple ways to join: speech, chat, reaction, or no social response.

### 8.6 Lunch, team events, and alcohol

- State time, cost, food options, accessibility, and voluntary status where relevant.
- Include non-alcoholic options without making them exceptional.
- Do not ask why someone declines food, alcohol, nightlife, sport, or an event.
- Do not equate attendance with loyalty or leadership potential.
- Keep work access available to people who leave early or do not attend.

Invitation: “A few of us are getting lunch at 12:30. Feel free to join us if you’d like.”

Decline: “Thanks for inviting me—I’m going to sit this one out. Have a good time.”

### 8.7 Manager and direct report

Managers should:

- ask about the work experience before personal life;
- never use small talk to discover medical, family, identity, or availability information;
- avoid public questions whose refusal would be visible;
- give explicit, credible opt-outs;
- model a brief answer and move on when the employee does not expand;
- separate social conversation from feedback, assignments, and documentation.

“How’s your workload this week?” is safer and more actionable than “You look exhausted—what’s wrong at home?” Ask the neutral work question routinely, not because you inferred a problem from someone’s appearance.

### 8.8 Senior colleague, client, or external partner

- Begin slightly more formal, then mirror their stated preference.
- Use the shared event, travel logistics, professional interests, or local practical information.
- Do not confuse hospitality with personal access.
- Avoid politics, jokes at a group’s expense, confidential comparisons, or complaints about colleagues.
- Move commercial questions into an explicit agenda.

### 8.9 Business travel and conferences

- Use the event content, venue, journey, city logistics, or professional interests.
- Do not ask whether someone traveled alone, where exactly they are staying, or what they are doing late at night.
- Respect jet lag, prayer, caregiving calls, dietary needs, disability access, and quiet time without requiring explanations.
- A conference badge, shared ride, dinner, or hotel bar does not reduce normal workplace boundaries.

### 8.10 Return from leave or a difficult period

If you know only that they were away:

- “Good to see you back. Would a quick update help, or would you rather settle in first?”
- “Welcome back. Let me know when you’d like a quick catch-up.”

If they disclosed loss or illness:

- “I’m sorry. Thank you for telling me.”
- “You don’t need to go into detail. What would be helpful at work?” if this is relevant to your role

Do not seek a full story, offer a diagnosis, compare grief, or share the information.

### 8.11 Regional English cues—hypotheses, not personality tests

Use these only as a cautious first hypothesis. A person’s preference and the team’s observed norm override every item.

- **United States and much of English-speaking Canada:** first names and upbeat, brief greetings are common in many organizations. “How’s it going?” often expects a short answer. Friendliness does not automatically mean friendship, agreement, or an invitation to disclose.
- **United Kingdom and Ireland:** “You all right?” or “All right?” often functions as a greeting. “Not bad” may be positive. Understatement, indirect exits such as “I should let you get on,” and established banter may occur. Newcomers and leaders should not copy teasing until safety and reciprocity are clear.
- **Australia and New Zealand:** many workplaces use first names, relaxed tone, and “How’s it going?” Surface informality does not remove hierarchy. Do not force “mate,” slang, profanity, self-mockery, or banter to prove cultural fit.
- **Continental Europe and global English teams:** there is no single European style. Directness, titles, silence, privacy, and the preferred amount of pre-work chat vary sharply. English may be everyone’s second or third language; use clear, literal sentences and less slang, wordplay, political humor, and culture trivia.

These are editorial, contributor-observed hypotheses, not evidence-backed claims about a population. Foreign companies in China may combine headquarters language, local hierarchy, industry practice, and one manager’s habits. Calibrate to the actual team rather than importing a national script.

### 8.12 Factory, laboratory, field, and shift work

- Safety, handover, hygiene, and protective-equipment procedures come before conversation.
- Do not distract someone operating equipment, handling a specimen, driving, lifting, counting, securing a site, or completing a critical handover.
- Keep shift-change contact practical and brief: “Morning—how did the handover go?” or “I’ll let you finish the check. See you on break.”
- Use designated break or safe areas for longer conversation.
- Noise, PPE, masks, radios, and hearing protection can change speech and eye-contact cues; do not interpret them as attitude.

### 8.13 Retail, hospitality, healthcare, logistics, and customer-facing work

- Do not prolong colleague small talk while a customer, patient, passenger, or delivery is waiting.
- Never use patient, guest, customer, or shipment information as conversation material.
- A customer’s warmth is not consent to private contact, flirting, or an invitation outside work.
- If a customer makes an identity-based or sexual comment, the organization’s duty to protect staff still applies.
- Reconnect during an actual lull: “That was a rush. You doing okay?” Then accept a brief answer.

## 9. Inclusion and accessibility

### 9.1 Introversion, anxiety, and limited social energy

- A quiet colleague is not rude, disengaged, or in need of fixing.
- Offer predictable, small formats instead of surprise public performance.
- Accept prepared phrases, written participation, and early exits.
- Practice one move at a time: greeting this week, one follow-up next week.
- Do not score personality through small-talk fluency.

Useful self-script: “I’m taking a quiet break today, but I’ll catch you later.”

### 9.2 Neurodiversity

- Do not require eye contact, facial matching, quick turn-taking, or interpretation of hints.
- When clarity matters, be direct and kind: “I need to focus now, but good talking with you.”
- Avoid sarcasm as the only signal that a topic is unwelcome.
- Give processing time and do not fill every pause.
- Ask about communication preferences, not diagnosis: “Would chat or a quick call be easier?”

### 9.3 Disability and health

- Speak to the person, not a companion or interpreter.
- Do not touch a person, mobility aid, service animal, or assistive device without permission.
- Offer help once and make “no” easy: “Would you like a hand with the door?”
- Do not ask for a medical explanation.
- Choose quieter, seated, captioned, accessible, or written options when organizing interaction.
- When unsure, start with a verbal greeting or a small wave. Do not initiate a hug or touch someone or their equipment without permission.
- Do not block an exit, move closer after someone steps back, or assume a past hug creates permanent consent.
- Do not treat speech speed or spoken fluency as a measure of understanding or competence. If someone uses typing, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), an interpreter, or another method, address the person directly and allow time. Ask: “Would you rather talk, type, or use chat?”

### 9.4 Second-language communication

Natural and simple beats idiomatic and fast.

- Use one question at a time.
- Avoid unexplained slang, culture trivia tests, and jokes about accent or grammar.
- Do not switch languages or “correct” speech without permission.
- Ask for clarification without blame: “I want to make sure I understood—did you mean Thursday or Friday?”
- If you are the learner: “English isn’t my first language—could you say that last part a little more slowly?”

Do not compliment someone as “surprisingly articulate” or ask them to perform their language for the group.

### 9.5 Names, pronouns, and identity language

- Use the name and pronouns a person provides without requesting an explanation.
- If you make a small mistake: “Sorry—she.” Correct and continue.
- If you do not know, use the person’s name or neutral wording rather than guessing.
- Do not make the person reassure you or discuss their identity in front of others.
- Follow applicable local law and company policy; dignity is not dependent on curiosity.

## 10. Boundaries, repair, and escalation

### 10.1 If someone asks you an unwanted question

Choose the level that fits:

- Light redirect: “Nothing exciting to report. How’s the launch going?”
- Boundary: “I keep that part of my life private.”
- Firm stop: “I’d rather not discuss that at work.”
- Exit: “I need to get back to my desk. See you later.”

You do not owe a reason, smile, alternate personal fact, or softer refusal.

### 10.2 If you asked something too personal

Use **Acknowledge → Release**:

“Sorry—that was too personal. You don’t need to answer.”

Stop there. Redirect only if the other person chooses to continue the exchange. Do not say “I was only being friendly,” describe your intentions at length, ask whether they are offended, or repeat the question in softer words.

### 10.3 If you used the wrong name or pronoun

Correct briefly: “Sorry, Mei.” Continue. If the harm was public or repeated, apologize privately and change the behavior. Do not turn the apology into a request for emotional labor.

### 10.4 If a joke or comment lands badly

- Stop; do not explain why it was funny.
- Say: “That wasn’t appropriate. I’m sorry.”
- Do not ask the targeted person to approve the apology.
- Change behavior and, if relevant, correct the record with the same audience.

### 10.5 If someone starts gossip

- “I don’t know enough to speculate.”
- “That sounds like their story to share.”
- “Let’s check with them about the work question directly.”

Do not reward gossip with follow-up questions, screenshots, emojis, or “just between us” promises.

### 10.6 If you are a bystander

Use the least escalatory move that is safe and effective:

- “Let’s change the subject.”
- “They don’t need to explain why they’re not drinking.”
- “That joke isn’t appropriate.”
- “Let’s ask them directly about the work question.”

Afterward, check privately what the affected person wants. Do not reveal their identity or take control of their story. Serious, repeated, power-imbalanced, or unsafe conduct may require policy-based escalation even when the speaker is a client or high performer.

### 10.7 Personal or romantic invitations

A personal invitation may be made once between peers when company policy allows it. A decline, no response, subject change, or vague postponement closes it. Do not repeat the invitation, move it to another channel, ask for a reason, or make work awkward afterward.

Managers and others who control work opportunities should not initiate romantic invitations to direct reports and must follow company relationship and conflict-of-interest policy. Friendliness, a shared meal, a conference, or an after-work event is not romantic consent.

### 10.8 If someone discloses something serious

Shift out of small-talk mode:

1. Thank them for telling you.
2. Do not investigate unless your formal role requires it.
3. Ask what they need now and explain any limits to confidentiality.
4. Use company support, safety, HR, safeguarding, emergency, or reporting procedures as appropriate.

For immediate danger or urgent medical risk, use local emergency procedures. This guide is not crisis or legal advice.

### 10.9 If behavior is repeated, discriminatory, sexual, coercive, or retaliatory

It is not “awkward small talk.” Record facts safely and use company policy, a manager outside the problem, HR/people team, union or employee representative, ombuds, ethics channel, or appropriate authority. The target is not required to personally educate or confront the person first.

## 11. Common failure modes

| Failure | What it feels like | Replacement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question machine | Interview or surveillance | One follow-up, then reflect or share |
| Topic roulette | No answer is really heard | Stay on one volunteered detail |
| Forced positivity | “Fine” becomes mandatory | Accept neutral or difficult answers without fixing |
| Biography mining | Identity becomes entertainment | Ask about present context, not origin story |
| Status networking | Warmth only flows upward | Include peers, junior staff, support staff, remote colleagues, and newcomers |
| Hidden work request | Friendly opening becomes a trap | State the work purpose early |
| Advice reflex | Disclosure becomes a problem to solve | Ask: “Would you like me to listen, or help think through options?” |
| Competitive sharing | Their story becomes your bigger story | Share briefly, then return attention |
| Endless exit | Three new topics after goodbye | End after the first close |
| Forced imitation | Slang, banter, or intimacy is copied too fast | Mirror formality lightly; keep your own natural voice |

## 12. Personal practice plan

### Week 1: make recognition easy

- Learn and use three colleagues’ names accurately.
- Offer one passing greeting per day.
- Practice one clean exit.

### Week 2: build reciprocity

- Use one shared-context opener.
- Listen for one detail and ask one follow-up.
- Share one sentence, then stop.

### Week 3: read and respect signals

- Notice who expands and who prefers brief contact.
- Exit on the first repeated non-expansion signal.
- Practice one boundary phrase for yourself.

### Week 4: broaden inclusion

- Greet someone outside your usual status or affinity circle.
- Try an async, low-pressure social touchpoint.
- Check whether your favorite topic assumes money, family, health, culture, or free time.

Track attempts, not popularity. A successful attempt can be: “I greeted them, noticed they were busy, and left kindly.”

## 13. Team and manager implementation

Teams may encourage connection, but should not mandate disclosure.

- Publish optional social windows and quiet alternatives.
- Make events accessible across time zones, caregiving, disability, diet, religion, cost, and alcohol preference.
- Rotate formats: pairs, written prompts, interest channels, short pre-meeting space, and no-social option.
- Keep decisions and valuable information in official work channels.
- Do not rank participation, camera use, event attendance, or sociability.
- Train leaders on power, privacy, harassment, and repair before asking them to facilitate.
- Do not use this etiquette guide to prevent workers from discussing pay, hours, working conditions, organizing, or other activity protected by applicable law.

Safer prompt: “Share a work tool or tip that helped you recently—or pass.”

Risky prompt: “Everyone must share an embarrassing secret so we can build trust.”

## 14. Final decision rule

When uncertain:

1. choose a less personal topic;
2. ask one question, not three;
3. give an easy exit;
4. believe the first boundary;
5. keep work access independent of social participation.

Warmth plus freedom is the standard. If you have to choose, protect freedom.
