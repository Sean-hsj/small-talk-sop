# Manager communication-style add-on — English

Use this after the [English SOP](sop.md) and [role × scenario matrix](role-scenario-matrix.md). It helps you adapt to a manager's observable communication preferences without diagnosing personality or performing a false version of yourself.

## 1. Scope and hard limits

This add-on is optional. It is for delivery choices such as length, order, pace, detail, and channel.

It applies to a direct manager, manager +1/+2, deputy or assistant manager, and dotted-line or matrix manager. Confirm the person's actual remit before applying any reporting-line assumption.

With a skip-level or rarely seen leader, you usually lack enough observations. Use the neutral default: brief, public-context, truthful, and easy to exit.

It does not authorize you to change facts, hide disagreement, flatter, imitate an accent or identity, accept intrusion, or tolerate abuse.

Do not assign MBTI, Enneagram, astrology, attachment, clinical, or other fixed labels. Do not infer a style from gender, age, nationality, race, disability, seniority, appearance, or one interaction.

A manager may communicate differently by task, channel, audience, language, energy, and deadline. Treat every description as a temporary working hypothesis.

This guide is not a performance-management tool. Managers must not use it to rank sociability, “culture fit,” loyalty, promotability, or access to mentoring.

## 2. Six observable dimensions

Observe repeated work behavior or ask directly. A person can sit anywhere on each range and move by context.

| Dimension | One end | Other end | Safe adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pace | Fast: quick exchanges and decisions | Deliberate: time to process | Lead with the point or send context early |
| Directness | Explicit: clear ask and answer | Indirect: context and softened signals | Confirm meaning without mocking either style |
| Focus | Task-first: outcome and next action | Relationship-first: connection and impact on people | Order the same truthful content differently |
| Level | Big picture: purpose and options | Detail: evidence, dependencies, edge cases | Start at the preferred altitude; keep backup detail |
| Decision style | Experiment: reversible trial | Risk control: predictability and safeguards | Name reversibility, risk, owner, and checkpoint |
| Social bandwidth | Expansive: longer informal exchange | Reserved: brief or low-frequency contact | Match contact length, not warmth or respect |

These are communication dimensions, not measures of character, competence, empathy, or leadership quality.

Reserved does not mean unfriendly. Fast does not mean careless. Deliberate does not mean indecisive. Indirect does not mean dishonest. Relationship-first does not mean unprofessional.

## 3. The OHAIR calibration loop

Use **OHAIR**: Observe → Hypothesize → Ask → Implement → Recalibrate.

### 3.1 Observe

Look for patterns across at least two comparable interactions when possible:

- Do they ask for the headline or the background first?
- Do they prefer live discussion, a short message, or a written pre-read?
- Do they ask about risks, people impact, evidence, or next actions?
- How do they close a useful exchange?
- Does the pattern change under time pressure?

Use work behavior, not personal identity. A single rushed Monday is weak evidence.

### 3.2 Hypothesize

Write a narrow, revisable guess:

> “For weekly project updates, Sam may prefer a three-line summary before details.”

Avoid a global verdict:

> “Sam is a dominant personality who hates detail.”

### 3.3 Ask

Ask a direct preference-check question. This is mandatory before relying on a high-impact assumption.

- “For these updates, would you prefer the headline first or the context first?”
- “Would a short note before our one-to-one help, or would you rather talk it through live?”
- “How much detail is useful here?”
- “When something is uncertain, do you want an early heads-up or a more developed option?”

Ask about a task, not their personality. Accept “it depends,” and ask what it depends on.

### 3.4 Implement

Try one low-risk change for a limited context. Keep facts, caveats, boundaries, and necessary disagreement intact.

> “I’ll try headline → risk → next step in the next two updates.”

Do not copy mannerisms, forced enthusiasm, bluntness, silence, or after-hours habits.

### 3.5 Recalibrate

Check the result instead of assuming success:

- “Was that level of detail useful?”
- “Should I make the next one shorter?”
- “I noticed deadlines change the format. What works best when time is tight?”

Update or discard the hypothesis. Recheck after a role, project, channel, or workload change.

## 4. Provisional profiles

Profiles combine dimensions for quick use. They are not people-types. Use only the dimensions that fit the current interaction.

### 4.1 Direct and fast

Likely cue: the manager asks for the conclusion, owner, or deadline early and closes once the next step is clear.

Better format: headline → status → risk → ask. Keep social openers genuine and brief.

Coffee:

> “Morning. How’s your day starting?”

If they answer briefly, close. Do not turn the coffee queue into a status report.

One-to-one transition:

> “Before we get into the agenda, how’s your week going? I have three items; the decision I need is on the second.”

Do not become rude, omit uncertainty, or pretend to have certainty they do not have.

### 4.2 Analytical and detail-oriented

Likely cue: the manager asks for sources, definitions, edge cases, comparisons, or dependencies.

Better format: short summary plus traceable detail. Separate fact, assumption, and open question.

After a weekend:

> “Hope you had a good weekend. No need for the full recap—did you get any downtime?”

For work:

> “Short answer: option B is lower risk. I brought the assumptions and comparison if you want them.”

Do not use a personal weekend detail as evidence for a personality conclusion.

### 4.3 Relational and people-first

Likely cue: the manager checks stakeholder impact, team energy, trust, or how a change will land.

Better format: acknowledge people and working relationships, then state the outcome and next step.

Lunch:

> “How has the team felt to you this week? We can keep lunch social if you’d rather.”

Work transition:

> “The change saves time, and support will need a clear handover. I suggest we brief them before Thursday.”

Warmth does not create permission to disclose gossip, health, family, or another employee’s feelings.

### 4.4 Strategic and big-picture

Likely cue: the manager asks why this matters, what options exist, or how it connects to a larger goal.

Better format: purpose → options → recommendation → explicit next step. Keep evidence available.

One-to-one:

> “The larger goal is reducing handoff time. I see two paths; I recommend the smaller pilot. May I confirm the owner and checkpoint?”

Small talk can begin broadly:

> “What part of the off-site discussion stayed with you?”

Do not leave ownership, dates, or disagreement vague just because the conversation is conceptual.

### 4.5 Structured and risk-aware

Likely cue: the manager asks for an agenda, sequence, approval path, risk, or decision record.

Better format: agenda → known facts → risk → mitigation → decision needed.

Before lunch or coffee:

> “I’m happy to keep this social. If you want to cover the launch too, I can send two questions and we can treat it as work time.”

Meeting start:

> “I sent three points. The first is information; the second needs a decision; the third is next week’s risk.”

Do not turn every casual encounter into a process or treat caution as fear.

### 4.6 Reserved or low-small-talk

Likely cue: the manager is courteous but rarely expands informal topics, prefers scheduled contact, or returns quickly to the task.

Better format: brief recognition, low-frequency contact, clear purpose, easy exit.

Coffee:

> “Morning. Hope the day goes smoothly.”

Return from leave:

> “Welcome back. I can send a short work catch-up whenever useful—no need to discuss the time away.”

Do not interpret brevity as rejection, coldness, or lack of sponsorship. Do not repeatedly “bring them out of their shell.”

### 4.7 High-energy or expansive

Likely cue: the manager thinks aloud, opens several threads, or uses visible enthusiasm.

Better format: engage at your natural energy, then summarize the agreed point.

> “There are several good ideas there. Let me play back the two actions I captured.”

You do not need to match volume, speed, disclosure, joking, touch, alcohol, or after-hours participation.

### 4.8 Indirect or context-first

Likely cue: the manager uses background, suggestions, pauses, or softened disagreement before the main point.

Better format: listen for context, then confirm the work meaning respectfully.

> “I heard two concerns: timing and support capacity. Should I pause the launch, or bring back a smaller option tomorrow?”

Do not claim agreement where none exists. Respectful confirmation is safer than guessing.

## 5. Context playbook

### 5.1 Coffee queue or kitchen

Default to one light line. Style affects length, not topic safety.

| Observed preference | Try | Exit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fast or reserved | “Morning—busy start?” | “I’ll let you get to it.” |
| Deliberate or analytical | “How is the week taking shape?” | “No rush—see you later.” |
| Relational or expansive | “How’s the team energy today?” | “Good catching up.” |

Do not corner the manager for a decision. State a real work need and schedule it.

### 5.2 One-to-one

Ask whether they want a social check-in, work agenda, or both. A recurring meeting is not automatic consent to personal disclosure.

> “Would you like to start with a quick check-in or go straight to the agenda?”

For a direct manager, lead with the decision. For a deliberate manager, send context early. For a relational manager, include stakeholder impact. For a strategic manager, connect the decision to the goal.

### 5.3 Weekend or leave return

Start broad and allow a non-answer:

> “Welcome back—hope the return is manageable. Want a short work catch-up now or later?”

If they volunteer a detail, follow at most one thread. Do not ask why they took leave, where they were, who they were with, or whether a health or family issue is resolved.

### 5.4 One-to-one lunch

Clarify purpose before adapting style:

> “Should we keep lunch social, use part of it for the project, or decide when we sit down?”

If work evaluation, feedback, or a decision appears unexpectedly, ask to move it into a documented work setting.

> “I want to give that proper attention. Can we put it on our one-to-one agenda and capture the next steps there?”

### 5.5 Two or three people

Do not let the manager’s preferred pace silence others. Name the format and rotate entry.

> “We have ten minutes. Could each of us give the headline, then we can choose one thread?”

Do not use your style knowledge to monopolize access or act as the manager’s interpreter unless that role is agreed.

### 5.6 Group lunch or lunch meeting

The manager’s social energy does not set a participation requirement for the group. Keep attendance, disclosure, food, alcohol, photos, and exit optional.

If there is an agenda, decisions, or assigned work, call it a lunch meeting and keep information in official channels.

### 5.7 Feedback or disagreement

Adapt sequence, not substance.

| Preference | Opening format |
| --- | --- |
| Direct or fast | “I disagree with option A because of the deadline risk. I recommend B.” |
| Deliberate or analytical | “I sent the evidence and assumptions. My conclusion is B; two uncertainties remain.” |
| Relational | “I share the goal. I’m concerned the current plan shifts unplanned work to Support.” |
| Strategic | “This may solve the local issue but work against the adoption goal. I recommend a pilot.” |
| Risk-aware | “The main risk is irreversible migration. A staged rollout keeps a rollback point.” |
| Indirect | “I may be missing context. I see a timing conflict; should we compare two alternatives?” |

Never soften a safety, legal, ethical, quality, or material business concern until it disappears.

## 6. Hybrid, changing, and stress styles

Most managers combine dimensions. Do not force a profile when a simple task preference is enough.

A manager may be expansive at lunch, detailed in reviews, direct during incidents, and deliberate with hiring. This is normal context change, not inconsistency to diagnose.

Under pressure, reduce guesswork:

> “For this incident, do you want a two-minute headline now and a written timeline after?”

Stress can explain a format change. It does not excuse yelling, threats, insults, retaliation, discrimination, stalking, sexual conduct, or unsafe demands.

## 7. Cross-cultural and second-language ambiguity

Directness, silence, turn-taking, titles, and enthusiasm vary across languages, teams, regions, and individuals. Accent or fluency is not a personality signal.

Use explicit process questions instead of national explanations:

- “Would you like me to be more concise, or add context?”
- “When you say ‘we can consider it,’ should I continue the current plan or prepare an alternative?”
- “I may have missed the nuance. Could you state the decision and owner?”

Allow processing time, written follow-up, captions, and clarification. Do not imitate speech, praise “surprisingly good English,” or treat a pause as agreement.

## 8. When the guess is wrong

Repair briefly and remove the label.

> “I assumed you wanted only the headline. I left out useful context—sorry. Would you prefer a short summary plus the assumptions next time?”

> “I read the pause as a no. I should have confirmed. What would you like me to do?”

Do not say, “But you’re usually that type.” Do not ask the manager to comfort you. Correct the current work record if the wrong guess affected a decision.

## 9. Unsafe behavior is not a style

The following are not preferences to accommodate:

- humiliation, shouting, threats, intimidation, retaliation, or discrimination;
- sexual comments, repeated personal invitations, touching, stalking, or private-pressure tactics;
- demands to hide facts, falsify records, bypass safeguards, or stay silent about a material concern;
- unequal mentoring, information, assignments, ratings, or access based on social similarity or participation;
- intrusive questions about identity, health, family, relationships, religion, money, or protected activity.

Keep a factual record when safe. Use policy, a manager outside the problem, HR/people team, union or employee representative, ombuds, ethics, safety, or an appropriate external authority.

The affected person is not required to adapt better, diagnose the manager, or confront them personally first.

## 10. Fast lookup

| If you repeatedly observe… | Try once | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Requests for the headline | Conclusion first | “Short enough?” |
| Requests for evidence | Summary plus source detail | “Which assumptions need more support?” |
| Questions about people impact | Name stakeholders and handover | “Is this the impact you meant?” |
| Questions about direction | Goal, options, recommendation | “Which outcome should govern?” |
| Questions about risk or sequence | Risk, mitigation, owner, checkpoint | “What assurance is missing?” |
| Brief informal replies | Short greeting and release | “Would scheduled updates work better?” |
| Context before the ask | Listen, paraphrase, confirm action | “Is that the decision?” |
| Several energetic threads | Engage, then summarize two actions | “Did I capture the priorities?” |

## 11. Personal mini-record

Keep only task-relevant preferences, not a secret personality dossier.

> Context: weekly project update
>
> Observation: asked for the decision and risk first in three updates
>
> Preference check: confirmed headline-first
>
> Trial: three-line summary with linked detail
>
> Recheck: after this project or if the format stops working

Do not record protected identity, health, family, rumor, emotion labels, or speculative motives. Follow company privacy and records rules.

## 12. Add-on release gates

Do not publish or teach a revision unless every answer is yes:

1. Are descriptions observable, provisional, task-specific, and revisable?
2. Is there a direct preference-check question and a recalibration loop?
3. Does it avoid diagnosis and identity, culture, gender, or age stereotypes?
4. Does it adapt format without changing truth, ethics, consent, or necessary disagreement?
5. Does it reject flattery, manipulation, masking pressure, and unequal access?
6. Does it preserve stricter privacy and power safeguards for managers?
7. Does it route abuse and unsafe conduct to support instead of calling it a style?
8. Can reserved, disabled, neurodivergent, second-language, and low-energy people use or decline it?
